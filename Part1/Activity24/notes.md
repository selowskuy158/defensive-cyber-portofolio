# A24 - Teach Your Family About Cybersecurity Topic of Your Choice

**Date:** 30 March, 2026
**Location:** Home, Mount Lawley, Perth, Western Australia

## Summary
This activity explores the challenge of communicating cybersecurity concepts to people without technical backgrounds, meaning that I taught my parents and younger sibling about phishing attacks and how to protect themselves. I chose phishing because it's very relevant to everyday internet use, and my family members all use email and social media regularly, so understanding this threat could directly help them stay safe.

## The Teaching Session

**Who I taught:** My parents (both in their late 50s) and my younger sister (16 years old). They have varying levels of technical knowledge - my dad uses email and online banking regularly, my mum is less comfortable with technology but uses email and Facebook, and my sister uses many online services for school and social reasons.

**What I covered:** I structured the session around three main topics: what phishing is, how to identify phishing attempts, and what to do if you receive a suspicious email or message.

## Topic 1: What is Phishing?

**The explanation:** I started by explaining that phishing is a technique attackers use to trick people into giving them passwords, personal information, or money by pretending to be someone trustworthy. The way it works is that attackers send an email or message that looks like it's from a bank, social media company, or other trusted organization, so meaning that most people's first reaction is to trust it and do what it asks.

**Real-world example:** I showed them an actual phishing email that I had received before (without clicking any links or downloading anything). The email appeared to be from "PayPal" saying that my account had suspicious activity and asking me to "verify my details" by clicking a link. I explained that this is a very common tactic, and that PayPal would never ask for passwords through email.

**Why it works:** I noticed that my family understood the concept better when I explained the psychology - that phishing works because attackers understand that people trust institutions they interact with regularly, and they're hoping people will act quickly without thinking carefully.

## Topic 2: How to Identify Phishing Attempts

**The visual signs:** I showed several examples of phishing emails and compared them to legitimate emails from real organizations. I highlighted some common signs:

1. **Sender email address:** Phishing emails often come from addresses that look similar to the real organization but aren't quite right. For example, a fake email might come from "paypa1.com" (with the number 1 instead of letter l) instead of "paypal.com". I made sure to point out that sometimes the display name says "PayPal" but the actual email address is different - you can see the real address if you hover over the name.

2. **Urgent language:** Many phishing emails use language like "Your account will be locked" or "Immediate action required," creating a sense of panic so that you'll act without thinking. I observed that my family tends to react emotionally to urgent messages, so this was an important warning for them.

3. **Links and buttons:** I showed them how to check where a link actually goes before clicking it. I explained that you can hover over a link to see the actual URL in the bottom corner of your browser - many phishing links will show a URL that's completely different from what the email text suggests.

4. **Grammar and spelling mistakes:** I noted that phishing emails sometimes have poor spelling and grammar, though increasingly attackers are improving the quality of their emails. However, official emails from banks and large companies usually have very professional writing, so mistakes should raise suspicion.

5. **Requests for personal information:** I emphasized that legitimate organizations will never ask you to send passwords, credit card numbers, or banking details through email. This is a very clear sign of a phishing attempt.

**Legitimate email comparison:** To make this clearer, I pulled up a legitimate email from a bank and compared it side-by-side with a phishing example. I noticed that the legitimate email had subtle security markers that my family hadn't seen before.

## Topic 3: What to Do If You Receive a Phishing Email

**Don't click anything:** I made very clear that the most important step is to not click any links in a suspicious email. Even clicking might confirm to attackers that your email address is active, so meaning that they might send you more phishing emails.

**Verify independently:** If an email claims to be from your bank and asks you to do something, I taught them to verify this independently. The correct way to do this is to go directly to the bank's website by typing the address into your browser (not using a link from the email), or to call the bank using a number you know is correct, rather than a number from the email.

**Report and delete:** I showed them how to report phishing emails as spam or phishing in Gmail and other email services. This helps email providers identify and block similar phishing attempts for other users. After reporting, they should delete the email.

**Tell someone:** I encouraged them to tell me or another trusted person if they receive suspicious emails, because sometimes it's hard to be certain on your own, and it's better to ask than to accidentally fall for the attack.

## Practical Exercise

To make the learning real, I set up a small activity where I showed them five different emails and asked them to identify which ones might be phishing. We went through each one together, and I was pleased to see that by the end they were much better at spotting the warning signs. My sister in particular picked up on the suspicious emails very quickly.

## My Sister's Perspective

My sister was interested to learn that phishing can happen on social media and messaging apps too, not just email. I explained that attackers sometimes send messages through Instagram, Facebook, or WhatsApp that look like they're from friends or organizations, asking you to click a link. This was very relevant to her because she uses these platforms daily.

## My Parents' Concerns

My parents asked several good questions: What if they accidentally click a link? (I explained that this usually isn't immediately dangerous, but they should not enter any information), How do they know if a website is safe? (I showed them how to look for the padlock symbol for HTTPS), and What if they're not sure whether an email is real? (I reinforced that asking me is always better than clicking).

## Observations and Lessons Learned

I observed that my family is more receptive to security teachings when it's personal and relevant, rather than abstract. When I showed them emails that looked like they could actually target them (fake bank notifications, suspicious PayPal claims), they were much more engaged than when I talked about general concepts.

I also learned that patience is important - my mum needed more time to understand the technical elements like URLs and email headers, and I had to explain several times before it really sank in. This taught me that different people need different approaches to learning about security.

## Conclusion

Teaching my family about phishing really helped me understand how to communicate security concepts to non-technical people. It's easy to assume that common security threats are obvious to everyone, but in reality, many people don't know how to protect themselves. This activity was valuable not just for improving my family's security, but also for developing my own skills at explaining cybersecurity in clear, practical terms. My family now has much better awareness of phishing, and I feel more confident that they'll be skeptical of suspicious emails rather than falling for attacks.
