# B6 - Attend 2 Cyber Ethics/Law Related Talks/Seminars

**Date:** 9 April, 2026

## Summary
This activity involves attending two talks or seminars related to cyber ethics and law. Understanding the legal and ethical side of cybersecurity is just as important as the technical side because cybersecurity professionals must operate within legal boundaries and make ethical decisions when handling sensitive data and security incidents.

## Seminars Attended

### 1. UWA Law and Technology Seminar - "Privacy Law in the Digital Age"
- **Date:** 25 March, 2026
- **Location:** UWA Law School, Perth
- **Summary:** This seminar was hosted by the UWA Law faculty and covered the current state of privacy law in Australia, with a focus on how the Privacy Act 1988 applies to digital data collection and storage. The speaker discussed how the Australian government is planning to update privacy laws to include stronger protections for personal data, especially after the major data breaches at Optus and Medibank. One key point was the discussion about the right to be forgotten, which is already a law in Europe under GDPR but does not exist yet in Australia. The speaker explained that this means Australians currently do not have the legal right to request companies to permanently delete their personal data, which is a significant gap in our privacy protections. I found this seminar very eye-opening because it showed me how the law is struggling to keep up with the pace of technological change.

### 2. Online Webinar - "Ethical Hacking: Where is the Line?"
- **Date:** 5 April, 2026
- **Location:** Online via YouTube Live (organised by SANS Institute)
- **Summary:** This webinar discussed the ethical boundaries of cybersecurity testing and hacking. The main topic was about responsible disclosure, meaning the process of reporting a vulnerability you find to the company that owns the system instead of exploiting it or publishing it publicly. The speaker explained that in Australia, there is no specific law that protects security researchers who find vulnerabilities, which means that even if you are trying to help, you could technically be charged under the Criminal Code Act 1995 for unauthorized access to a computer system. The webinar also discussed bug bounty programs where companies actually pay people to find and report vulnerabilities, and how this is becoming a more accepted and legal way for security researchers to test systems. I learned that ethics in cybersecurity is not always black and white, and that there are many grey areas that require careful judgement.
